Think of insects: many have a larval form built for eating and an adult form built for travelling and reproducing. Machine learning models could work the same way.

The most accurate models are often large neural networks or ensembles of multiple models, but they are too expensive to run for millions of users.

Hinton and colleagues trained a small model to copy the big model's output probabilities, not just the correct labels. These "soft targets" contain extra information: for example, a picture of a BMW is rarely mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still far more likely than mistaking it for a carrot. A setting called temperature makes these differences easier for the small model to learn by producing softer probability distributions.

On the MNIST handwritten-digit dataset, a small network trained normally made 146 errors, but trained with soft targets it made only 74, close to the large network's 67.

This idea, now called knowledge distillation, lets us transfer knowledge from a large model to a smaller one, and it is used today to build faster, more efficient AI models that are easier to deploy.
